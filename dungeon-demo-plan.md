# Procedural Voxel Dungeon — Implementation Plan

> Implementation: **The Hollow Crown**, playable in this directory. See `README.md` for controls and run instructions, and `VERIFICATION.md` for acceptance results. The implementation decisions recorded below supersede the initial prototype choices where noted.

### Combat / ECS extension

The demo now uses a typed, indexed entity/component registry for characters, generated world objects, render batches, projectiles, and hit particles. Player and enemies share movement, pathfinding, door capabilities, attack timing, damage/death, collision adapters, and rig animation; input and AI provide intents. Generated data is registered by reference so navigation and interaction state remain consistent.

Seed 8 wandering skeletons, 4 cover-seeking ranged goblins, and 3 packs of 3 flanking kobolds. Skeletons attack living species; goblins evade the player and other species; kobolds share alerts and approach distinct target slots. Preserve corpses and use bounded procedural blood/bone-dust effects. Enemy click/hold locks pursuit and repeats melee strikes until death; floor/WASD/Space and modal transitions cancel the command. Clicking an unlocked door approaches and opens it; capable actors can also open doors during pursuit. Locks remain progression-controlled.

## 1. Demo goal

Build a playable browser-based dungeon using **Babylon.js and an HTML canvas**, with a visible, controllable voxel adventurer viewed through an overhead third-person camera.

All geometry is generated in code. Surface patterns, wear, liquid animation, fire, and magical effects are generated by shaders. No imported models, image textures, sprite sheets, or animation assets.

**Proposed experience:** explore a ruined subterranean fortress, cross a flooded chamber, descend toward a lava forge, activate an altar, and unlock the throne-room exit. A complete run should take approximately 5–10 minutes.

## 2. Visual direction

**Art target: a sculptural voxel dungeon rendered like a dark graphic novel.** Use Hades, Darkest Dungeon, and Mike Mignola's artwork as complementary visual references.

### Reference breakdown

| Reference | Qualities to carry into the demo | Implementation consequences |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hades | Readable elevated room compositions, strong architectural framing, saturated accents, and a clearly separated hero | Compose each room around a landmark; reserve visual space around paths; use localized color and responsive movement |
| Darkest Dungeon | Oppressive gothic spaces, distressed surfaces, heavy contours, and warm light surrounded by darkness | Use narrow pools of torchlight, desaturated masonry, angular ruins, and selective ink-like surface marks |
| Mike Mignola | Bold black shadow masses, simplified angular silhouettes, economical detail, and monumental occult architecture | Use broad lighting bands, deep near-black recesses, stepped forms, oversized arches, and sparse geometric symbols |

### Shape, color, and composition

- Chunky voxel silhouettes with exaggerated proportions: broad shoulders on the adventurer, massive door frames, tall throne backs, and heavy altar slabs.
- Build recognizable forms at room-view scale before adding small voxels. Avoid uniform cube noise across every surface.
- Use a limited base palette: charcoal, desaturated slate, dirty bone, and muted burgundy. Add blue-green water, amber torchlight, vermilion lava, and restrained violet altar energy.
- Give each room one dominant accent: teal flooded hall, ember-red forge, violet sanctuary, and tarnished gold/burgundy throne room.
- Uneven masonry, broken pillars, arches, stepped platforms, and recessed pools create deliberate architectural compositions.
- Group skulls, chains, candles, bones, treasure, and shields into readable clusters rather than scattering them uniformly.
- Preserve large quiet surfaces and dark recesses so silhouettes and landmarks carry the image.
- Open-top rooms and camera-facing wall cutaways keep the player visible. Frame important landmarks so they read from all supported camera orientations.

### Graphic shading rules

- Use two or three broad diffuse-light bands, deep colored shadows, and restrained highlights rather than smooth photorealistic gradients.
- Favor crisp but antialiased cast shadows and authored-looking procedural shadow accents in recesses. Use 2048px high-quality PCF shadows, derivative-aware lighting bands, MSAA, and FXAA to avoid staircase edges.
- Add selective dark silhouettes/creases around the character, major props, and architectural boundaries. Do not outline every voxel edge.
- Apply sparse procedural chips, cracks, and short hatch-like marks, anchored to object/world space and faded at distance to prevent shimmering.
- Keep water reflective-looking through graphic highlights and moving ripple bands; keep lava bright through contrasting dark crust and narrow emissive fissures.
- Limit bloom to small highlights so it does not erase black shapes or flatten the graphic contrast.
- The player, interaction points, and traversable surfaces must remain readable in grayscale; color reinforces navigation but is not its only cue.

Use a logical tile grid for layout and collision, with smaller voxel units for visual construction. An initial scale is one tile per world unit and four visual voxels per tile. This gives chunky detail without requiring every small cube to be simulated individually.

## 3. Technical approach

### Rendering and application

- Strict TypeScript ES modules, Babylon.js `Engine`/`Scene` targeting WebGL2, and one responsive HTML canvas. Application code, tests, and Vite configuration are type-checked before production builds.
- Use modular `@babylonjs/core` imports; add matching-version materials packages only where used, and load `@babylonjs/inspector` during development.
- Pin Babylon.js package versions so shader integration remains reproducible.
- DOM overlay for controls, health, interaction prompts, seed, and restart/regenerate actions.
- Use `engine.runRenderLoop` for rendering, with a bounded fixed-step accumulator for gameplay updates. Integrate the collision movement cadence explicitly with Babylon's scene update and world-matrix refresh behavior; verify repeated movement solves rather than assuming a generic substep loop is safe.
- Clamp elapsed time after tab suspension to prevent large movement jumps.
- Seeded randomness, with independent streams for layout, decoration, and material variation.
- Keep dungeon data separate from Babylon.js objects so generation and reachability can be validated without rendering.
- Use Babylon's picking/rays, animation utilities, shadows, glow, and inspector where appropriate. Dungeon generation, voxel meshing, progression, and camera cutaways remain custom systems.

### Geometry

- Store solid occupancy, floor elevation, liquid regions, and interactive entities separately.
- Build static architecture as chunked exposed-face meshes through Babylon's `VertexData`; omit faces enclosed by other solid voxels.
- Batch static decorative pieces using thin instances, grouped by material and spatial chunk; refresh batch bounds after building or changing them.
- Generate doors, animated character parts, and moving mechanisms as separate meshes organized under `TransformNode` hierarchies.
- Build simplified, invisible collision meshes from the same dungeon data. Thin-instance render geometry is not the collision source of truth.
- Generate props using reusable code recipes: boxes, stepped profiles, cylinders where useful, and voxel arrangements.
- Use explicit door and stair connectors between floor regions. Avoid vertically overlapping walkable floors in the first demo, keeping collision and overhead visibility manageable.

### Procedural materials

- Implement banded surface lighting and procedural patterns using Babylon's `CustomMaterial`, preserving standard lighting, shadow reception, fog, and thin-instance support. This replaces the initial CellMaterial/NodeMaterial prototype approach with a single shared lit-material path.
- Prove lighting, shadow reception, instance transforms, and cutaway compatibility in the first-room prototype before applying the material throughout the dungeon. Use built-in standard/PBR materials for debugging or limited accents where their appearance fits.
- Use dedicated `ShaderMaterial` effects for liquids, flame, embers, and altar energy; wire required lighting/fog behavior explicitly.
- Generate surface variation from object/world coordinates, quantized noise, and mathematical patterns. Runtime shader-generated textures are allowed; imported image textures are not.
- Babylon's procedural texture library can supply noise, brick, wood, and fire foundations where useful. Tune these to the graphic style rather than adopting their default look.
- Stone: broad block-color variation, dark mortar, sparse angular chips, damp staining, and limited moss.
- Wood: simplified grain bands, plank seams, and darkened edges.
- Metal: mostly flat dark tones, tarnish patches, and narrow stylized highlights.
- Water: shallow colored surface, graphic ripple bands, and shoreline foam from generated boundary data. Start opaque or minimally translucent to preserve readability and avoid complex sorting/refraction.
- Lava: large dark crust islands, animated glowing fissures, and occasional procedural sparks.
- Altar: geometric rune patterns, hard-edged emissive bands, and restrained pulsing light.
- Prefer custom liquid shaders for the intended look. Babylon's library water/lava materials are optional foundations, not asset-free drop-ins: water expects a bump texture, and lava expects noise/diffuse textures. Generate those inputs on the GPU if those materials are used.
- Account for instance transforms in shader coordinates so repeated objects render correctly.
- Runtime render targets for generated patterns, shadows, or post-processing are allowed; the asset restriction applies to authored visual assets. Do not load remote material snippets or image-based environment maps.

### Lighting and performance

- Low hemisphere fill, one primary shadow-casting directional light, and a bounded pool of nearby local lights. Preserve deep shadow masses without losing the walkable floor.
- Use Babylon's `ShadowGenerator`; tune shadow resolution, bias, and filtering against the graphic style rather than defaulting to very soft shadows.
- Flames and lava combine emissive surfaces with selected local lights; emissive color alone does not illuminate surrounding geometry.
- Mostly non-shadow-casting torch lights, instanced flame geometry, and pooled particles. Use generated particle shapes/shaders rather than imported particle sprites.
- Cap effective render resolution with engine hardware scaling and expose low/high quality presets for shadows and effects.
- Use a selective `GlowLayer` for lava cracks, flames, and runes. Add further post-processing only after the base image is performant and readable.
- Use the Inspector and engine/scene instrumentation to track frame time, draw calls, mesh counts, and active lights.

## 4. Procedural dungeon generation

Start with a **room connectivity graph**, then embed it into the tile grid. This makes the demo’s required content and progression reliable.

1. Grow an irregular connected cluster of thirteen rooms and assign their roles from the seed. Keep the throne at a gated leaf; the altar must be accessible before opening that gate.
2. Add seeded branches and optional loops. The route and landmark order vary between runs.
3. Place rectangular, chamfered, and cross-shaped footprints, reserve clearance, and connect them with offset corridors and doorways.
4. Assign two elevation bands connected by properly sized stairs.
5. Place water and lava basins with safe crossings. Reserve walkable approaches before decorating.
6. Place landmarks, reliquaries, and role-specific furnishings using seeded placement with reserved interaction approaches. Directional furnishings face the room center; retry positions where the inward rotation cannot fit. Include butchery, blacksmith, bakery, and lizard-stable rooms and shader-generated royal portraits.
7. Place interaction points, checkpoints, and progression state.
8. Validate walkability including prop footprints, stair links, door clearance, all interaction approaches, and the completion route.
9. Retry invalid candidates deterministically, with a bounded attempt count and explicit failure diagnostic. Default loads and browser refreshes choose fresh seeds; explicit seed links reproduce a world when opened.

The expanded demo contains **thirteen rooms** with variable overall extents. The minimap fits the generated bounds automatically; render and shadow work remain localized around the player.

Reachability validation must model door states and altar activation, not just treat all doors as open. The altar must be reachable before the gate it unlocks; the throne-room exit must become reachable afterward.

## 5. Required dungeon features

| Feature | Procedural construction | Demo behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doors | Thick plank panels, iron bands, voxel frames | Open/close with interaction; collider follows state; closing is blocked while the player occupies the doorway |
| Stairs | Repeated stone steps, optional side walls | Connect actual elevations; use a smooth collision ramp beneath the visible steps |
| Water | Recessed tiled basin, shader surface, stone banks | Shallow sections slow movement; a bridge or stepping-stone route preserves access |
| Lava | Cracked basin, animated emissive surface | Damages the player on contact; always has a safe route around or across it |
| Throne | Stepped dais, oversized block-built seat, armrests, metal details | Main visual landmark and final interaction point |
| Altar | Stone plinth, candles, floating voxel fragments, procedural rune glow | Activates on interaction, changes its effect, and unlocks the throne-room gate |
| Torches/braziers | Generated brackets or bowls with shader flames | Flicker and light nearby features |
| Pillars/arches | Stacked blocks, capitals, broken variants | Define room silhouettes and add collision obstacles |
| Treasure and ruins | Chests, barrels, crates, bones, rubble, shields | Mostly decorative; at least one chest can open as an optional interaction |
| Butchery | Chopping blocks, cleavers, hanging cuts, iron hooks | Distinct seeded working-room layout |
| Blacksmith | Furnace, anvils, tool bench, quenching tub | Animated fire and warm local lighting |
| Bakery | Brick oven, scored loaves, bread rack, flour sacks | Animated hearth and recognizable baked goods |
| Lizard stable | Stalls, straw, feed trough, tack rack, two saddled lizards | Procedural breathing, head turns, and tail movement |
| Royal portraits | Seeded 32 × 40 pixel art rendered in a lit shader, with a 128 × 160 detailed mode | Reusable eye-level inspection camera, identity caption, and return controls; paintings follow wall cutaways |

Decoration placement respects reserved routes, stair landings, door swing space, and interaction clearance. Every generated dungeon contains all six requested landmark types: doors, stairs, water, lava, throne, and altar.

## 6. Player, camera, and interaction

### Character

- Generate a recognizable adventurer from voxel body parts: head, torso, boots, arms, helmet, and a small shield or backpack.
- Animate idle, push-off/start, travel, and planted-stop poses mathematically. Drive stride from measured movement, blending torso lean, arms, feet, and cape follow-through.
- Use an invisible ellipsoid collision proxy with Babylon's `moveWithCollisions` against simplified collision meshes. The visible voxel character follows the proxy.
- Implement grounded state, downward motion, camera-relative velocity, and stable support heights explicitly; camera gravity settings do not implement a third-person character controller.
- Use ramp colliders beneath visual stair steps and downward support probes to maintain stable contact. Verify wall sliding, stair descent, and movement at different frame rates early.
- Keep collision independent of visual voxel detail so chipped blocks do not snag movement.
- Start with Babylon's built-in collisions. Havok and `PhysicsCharacterController` are a later option if dynamic bodies or richer physical interactions become part of the demo.

### Controls

- **WASD / arrow keys:** camera-relative movement.
- **Left-click the floor:** follow a clearance-checked tile route to the destination ring. Hold left mouse to steer continuously; keyboard movement takes priority. Closed doors and lava block automatic routes.
- **Space:** cancel click travel and ease to a stop.
- **E:** interact with the closest valid object in range and line of sight.
- **Shift:** move faster.
- Normal travel is 4.8 units/second; running is 7.4. Use short frame-rate-independent acceleration/deceleration and arrival braking, with immediate intent reset on pause, inspection, focus loss, and regeneration.
- **Q / R:** rotate camera in 90° increments.
- **Mouse wheel:** zoom within a limited range.
- **Escape:** pause and show controls.
- On-screen actions: restart the current seed or generate a new dungeon.
- Inspection uses a reusable target descriptor and camera controller. Pause player input, show the selected target at eye level, enable its detailed presentation, and restore the previous view on E/Escape/close. Suppress native text actions for held gameplay keys while allowing text-field editing.

### Camera

- Use Babylon's `ArcRotateCamera` with a restricted elevation approximately 50–60° above the floor and a smooth player-follow target. Disable unrestricted default orbit/pan input in favor of the listed controls.
- Start with a narrow perspective field of view around 35° for an isometric-like composition while retaining depth. Tune camera distance and room dimensions together.
- Smoothly follow a target near the character's torso; gently follow elevation changes and keep facing independent of camera rotation.
- Start around 10–14 world units from the target and tune against corridor dimensions.
- Omit ceilings and retract camera-facing upper wall sections geometrically, preserving the lower silhouette and keeping invisible collision walls intact.
- Use the same retracted geometry for shadow rendering, ensuring visible walls and shadows agree without separate clipping shaders.
- Built-in camera collision can constrain the camera but does not provide wall cutaways. Keep overhead visibility as an explicit custom system.
- Show a subtle ground marker beneath the player for orientation.

### Minimal game loop

Spawn → explore → traverse water and stairs → avoid lava → activate altar → enter throne room → interact with throne to complete the demo.

Include a small health meter, lava damage, and respawn at a safe checkpoint. Combat, enemy AI, inventory, and equipment progression are potential follow-up work rather than dependencies for this exploration demo.

## 8. Implementation milestones

### Milestone 1 — Playable movement, camera, and art prototype

Build the Babylon engine/scene, render loop, input, generated character, collision proxy, and overhead camera in a small code-generated test room. Include a corridor, doorway, stairs, and small water/lava basins immediately. Enable the development Inspector.

Establish the limited palette, banded lighting, a large architectural shadow, selective contours, and one warm/cool accent pairing. Test the production surface shader on an animated door and a thin-instance batch, including shadow reception and a wall cutaway. Capture reference views from the playable camera before expanding the generator.

**Done when:** movement is camera-relative, the player remains visible, wall sliding works, and stairs can be traversed in both directions without jitter. The room reads as graphic dark fantasy at gameplay scale: clear silhouette, deep shadow masses, restrained detail, and readable paths in both color and grayscale.

### Milestone 2 — Seeded dungeon layout

Implement room graphs, spatial placement, corridors, elevation connectors, and validation. Add chunked architecture meshes and a seed UI.

**Done when:** the same seed reproduces the same dungeon, all required rooms exist, and every intended route is traversable.

### Milestone 3 — Interactive exploration loop

Implement doors, altar activation, the locked gate, throne completion, water slowdown, lava damage, and safe respawn.

**Done when:** a full run can be completed, interactions cannot occur through walls, doors cannot trap the player, and a death/reset cannot leave progression in an impossible state.

### Milestone 4 — Procedural visual pass

Expand the approved first-room look across stone/wood/metal shaders, water and lava animation, throne and altar detailing, torches, architectural variants, props, and character animation. Complete camera obstruction cutaways, selective outlines, and per-room palette variants.

**Done when:** all requested landmarks are visually distinct, there are no imported visual assets, and the player and safe route remain readable throughout the dungeon. Broad shadow shapes and architectural silhouettes dominate over surface noise; glow does not wash out the graphic contrast.

### Milestone 5 — Performance and release checks

Tune chunk sizes, instancing, light limits, particle budgets, shadows, and pixel ratio. Add pause, resizing, quality settings, and reliable resource disposal on regeneration.

**Done when:** the acceptance checks below pass and measured performance meets the agreed target on a recorded reference machine/browser.

## 9. Acceptance and verification

- Automated generation checks across at least 100 seeds: deterministic output, required features, valid stair links, accessible interactions, and solvable progression.
- Targeted movement checks: corners, narrow doorways, stair transitions, closed gates, water boundaries, and lava respawn.
- Play through several materially different seeds from entrance to completion.
- Confirm camera visibility in narrow corridors, at both stair ends, and around tall props from every supported camera orientation.
- Confirm shader patterns remain correctly positioned on instanced props and animated doors.
- Review gameplay screenshots at thumbnail size and in grayscale: the player, exits, hazards, and room landmark should remain distinguishable.
- Check that outlines do not form a grid around every voxel, procedural hatching does not shimmer during movement, and cutaway walls do not leave mismatched shadows.
- Check current desktop Chrome and Firefox with WebGL2; show a clear message when unavailable.
- Verify resizing, tab suspension, pause/resume, and repeated dungeon regeneration.
- Inspect network requests to confirm there are no external model, image, texture, or animation assets.
- Profile a target of 60 FPS at 1080p on the chosen desktop reference machine, with a lower-quality mode for slower hardware. Record frame time, draw calls, triangles, and memory rather than assuming the target is achieved.
- Repeated regeneration must release geometries, materials, and render targets without unbounded memory growth.

## 10. First build priority

Implement **one polished, code-generated Babylon.js room containing the player, a door, stairs, and small water/lava basins** before expanding the dungeon generator. This proves the camera, collision, elevation handling, shader approach, and visual scale together. It also establishes the Hades / Darkest Dungeon / Mignola-inspired art target through actual gameplay-camera screenshots, rather than postponing the style until final polish.
